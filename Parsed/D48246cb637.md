GUIDELINES FOR SUBMITTING AN INNOVATION PROJECT 
 
There are two ways to submit an innovation project to MSF Scientific Days: as an abstract or a demo. 
Please see “What is an innovation project?” for guidance on which format is appropriate for your project. 
 
Content 
Your abstract should give enough information about your innovation project clearly and concisely. 
  
Introduction 
Your introduction should describe the background to your project:  
• 
What challenge or opportunity did your project address and why does it matter to MSF? 
• 
What change did you expect it make? 
 
Methods  
Your methods section should describe how you collected and analysed your results: 
• 
How did you plan to compare the resulting change against what exists already? 
• 
What indicators of success did you measure and how did you analyse them? 
 
Results 
Describe the data used to evaluate your project and what the results showed: 
• 
What change have you seen from the work you’ve done? 
• 
Did it offer a better, cheaper, faster, or easier solution than what exists? Were results as expected? 
• 
Describe other factors you considered that might have had an impact on your results 
• 
If the project changed, what evidence informed the change and what was the result? 
• 
Give actual numbers as well as percentages, with clear denominators; report standard deviations 
(SDs) with means, interquartile ranges (IQRs) with medians, and 95% CIs and p-values where 
appropriate.  
 
Conclusion 
Describe what you learned, the implications of your work, and what should happen next: 
• 
Did the project achieve its objectives? If ongoing, what are the next steps; if stopped early, why? 
• 
Whether failure or success, what did you learn from this and what are the implications (potential 
impact) of your work, for practice, policy, programmes or advocacy for MSF or others? 
• 
What should happen next in this area of innovation / within your project? 
 
Ethics 
All abstracts must include an ethics statement: 
• 
For innovation projects not involving human subjects or their data, please consult the Innovation 
Ethics Framework and indicate in the submission system if it has been followed. 
• 
Projects involving human subjects or their data must have appropriate ethics oversight from an 
ethics review board or the relevant MSF Operational Centre Medical Director. See the MSF Ethics 
Review Board (ERB) guidance.

Application Process 
Support  
Assistance is available to review/assist in abstract writing. If you require this, please contact 
scientificday@london.msf.org as early as possible.  
Abstract review 
Selected abstracts are usually offered an oral or poster presentation. However, where the Editorial 
Committee considers that an abstract is particularly suited to an alternative format, authors may be offered 
this option. Authors will be responsible for developing and producing the content and materials, with 
guidance from the Scientific Days team.  
Poster authors may also be offered an opportunity to produce a video or give a one-minute presentation to 
support their poster.  
Abstract editing  
Abstracts accepted for oral presentations will be edited. The aim is to ensure that presenters are fully 
prepared to meet questions that may be raised during the conference and to ensure that MSF research is 
presented clearly and appropriately.  
Authors must be available to revise abstracts according to the conference timeframes. Please note that 
the offer of an oral presentation or poster involves commitment by authors to meet deadlines and to 
respond to points raised by the Editorial Committee.  
Abstracts will be edited for language readability, scientific clarity, consistency, and adherence to abstract 
guidelines (see Content section above).  
Edited abstracts will include questions/points raised by the Editorial Committee – these must be addressed 
in the abstract revision.  
Presentation editing 
Presentation slides will be reviewed to ensure that guidelines are met – in particular around ethics 
statements, image use (author and copyright information included, including for maps; consent and ethics 
of using images of people or patients, particularly children), and clarity/accuracy of language. 
Oral presentation practice  
To ensure all oral presenters are fully prepared to present to both an in-person audience and an online 
audience of thousands, public-speaking training is available ahead of MSF Scientific Days and is highly 
recommended for anyone who would find it useful.  
All presenters are required to take part in at least one practice session with the MSF Scientific Days team 
and/or Editorial Committee representatives, regardless of their level of presentation experience. These 
sessions help us ensure that presentations are clear, engaging, appropriate for the audience, and meet the 
standards expected of the event. MSF Scientific Days has a strong reputation for high-quality 
presentations, and the team reserves the right to require additional practice where needed.  
Content sharing  
All abstracts, posters, slides, and videos of oral presentations will be shared on the MSF Science Portal. 
Posters will be shared in advance of the conference and tweeted via @msfsci. Highlights of the day will be 
written up for blogs and potentially further publication.

Conflicts of interest  
You will be asked to declare any conflicts of interest. Failure to disclose these might lead to withdrawal of 
abstracts or presentations from MSF Scientific Days. All conflicts of interest will be published in the 
conference booklet.  
All authors must disclose any financial and personal relationships with other people or organisations that 
could inappropriately influence (bias) their work. All submissions must include disclosure of all relationships 
that could be viewed as presenting a potential or actual conflict of interest.  
If there are no conflicts of interest, authors should tick the box to state that there are none.  
Authorship  
Please ensure that the names of all those who qualify for authorship are included in the submission. Note, 
we would expect to see authors from the countries where the research is conducted.  
If your abstract is selected for presentation, we strongly encourage teams to consider nominating someone 
directly involved in the operational response or programme to present the work. MSF Scientific Days aims 
to bring operational perspectives to the forefront, and we particularly welcome presentations from 
colleagues who can speak to the context, relevance, and application of the innovation in practice.

What is an innovation project? 
 
In MSF, an innovation can be the creation and implementation of new or novel products, services, or 
strategic approaches. Or an innovation can mark an advance on an existing product, service, or strategy.  
 
Any MSF innovation project is designed to benefit or improve an aspect of healthcare delivery either for 
patients, communities, or MSF staff. This doesn’t mean the project has to be medical in nature, but it must 
improve the way MSF provides medical care (for example, developing new systems for regenerating 
boreholes).  
 
What kind of innovation projects can be submitted as an abstract? 
 
We welcome abstracts from the initiation, development, or implementation stages (see diagram below). 
However, we don’t accept projects which are at the idea or design stage. The MSF Scientific Days are 
special because they focus on improving our work in the field through evidence and rigorous evaluation / 
analysis.  
 
What does this mean? It means that, ideally, you have developed and tested your innovation (i.e. gathered 
data to analyse whether it worked and the impact it had or could have), and can submit an abstract based 
on the outcomes, how you achieved them and what they might mean for MSF’s work. A good example can 
be found here. 
 
We are equally interested in ‘failed’ projects and successful ones. We know that not all innovations achieve 
the hoped-for outcome or impact - we need to ‘fail forward’ and learn.  
 
You can submit an abstract for a project at the Initiation or Development stage as long as you can draw 
conclusions from analysis of data. These learnings could be in the form of a new process for MSF or a new 
perspective on a field challenge. Here are two examples: 
 
Luc has an idea of how to solve a field challenge, but through his analysis of that challenge, he finds that it 
is actually a symptom of a wider problem. He then explores this wider problem and generates a new idea of 
how to solve it. Because Luc’s project already has relevance for MSF’s work and is based on evidence and 
analysis, he can submit it as an abstract as long as he can describe how he obtained the evidence and 
what analysis he did. 
 
Pascale is creating a new tool for nurses in the field. She tests a new approach to developing this tool and 
subsequently discovers a much more effective way to gather and communicate MSF nurses’ requirements 
to the design team, meaning time and money are saved. If she can demonstrate through data how that 
process has improved upon what already exists, she can submit it as an abstract. See an example of this 
here.

What kind of innovation projects can be submitted as a demo? 
 
The demo session is for innovative projects that are ‘work in progress’, where you might not yet have 
results from testing or implementation. It’s a great opportunity to present your project more informally and 
get feedback from the MSF Scientific Days audience.
