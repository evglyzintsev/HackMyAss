SUMMARY
In August 1995, for the first time in its history, North Korea launched a call for inter-
national aid following the destruction of its harvests by flooding. Following the break-
down of the Soviet Union, its main source of support, the planned economy of this 
closed-off country gradually collapsed. The population, completely dependent on the 
regime’s arbitrary resource management, experienced shortages of energy, food, and 
medicines.
The countries involved in the Korean crisis (regional powers and the United States of 
America, USA) were anxious to avoid a collapse of North Korea, which would impact on 
the entire region. They were encouraging peace negotiations between the two Koreas 
and a freezing and dismantling process of North Korea’s nuclear arms. The United 
Nations responded to this appeal with the delivery of millions of tonnes of aid, thereby 
entering years of an ongoing bargaining process linking the progress of negotiations to 
the allocation of aid.
From October to December 1995, an international Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) 
team, composed of staff from the Belgian, French, and Dutch sections, set up a pro-
gramme in North Korea providing epidemiological monitoring of diarrhoea and distrib-
uting medicines and medical material. 
In January 1997, MSF again received authorisation to open a barley seed distribution 
programme, and then, from June 1997 on, over several provinces, a one-year pro-
gramme providing support to improve nutritional therapeutic care to malnourished 
children, including training and the distribution of medical material, medicines, and 
drinking water to health centres. 
For one and a half years, 13 MSF international staff from three different sections fol-
lowed the example of their colleagues who had passed through the country in 1995, 
working under tight surveillance while being unable to evaluate needs based on objec-
tive criteria or check the destination of their aid. Nonetheless, some of them were 
encouraged by certain signs and events. There was the organisation of case manage-
ment for a group of children totally excluded by the system, for example.
Meanwhile, the MSF France team in China, having received information on the outcome 
of North Korean refugees in China, carried out two visits (in April and August 1998) to 
the Sino- Korean border. The refugees provided them with descriptions of a large-scale 
famine, violence, and arbitrary rule in the country. They also reported that international 
aid was distributed as a priority to the army and to individuals identified as “socially 
useful” and faithful to the regime. This situation was also reported by South Korean 
organisations providing assistance to refugees and by the international press, which 
started conveying echoes of famine in North Korea.
In August 1998, after several months of negotiation between MSF and the North 
Korean authorities on the renewal of their Memorandum of Understanding (MOU), 
Pyongyang made only one proposition: the organisation of an importation programme

for basic materials that would allow the regime to rehabilitate the country’s pharma-
ceutics industry. MSF refused, considering that this type of activity did not fall within 
the mandate of a medical humanitarian organisation, and withdrew from North Korea.
Throughout this period, the occasions when MSF spoke out in public, whilst conducted 
in the name of the entire movement, caused disagreement between sections and/or 
between headquarters and the field teams. As it turns out, there are contradictions 
within these arguments.
In December 1997, during a press conference, MSF publicly insisted on the necessity of 
providing emergency assistance to the North Korean health system. 
In April 1998, in the international press, MSF described the restrictions imposed on its 
teams during their activities carried out for the North Korean population and expressed 
their doubts on the final destination of their aid, but stated that it did not have any solid 
proof for denying or confirming the existence of a famine. At the same time, the inter-
national press circulated witness accounts of North Korean refugees, received from the 
MSF team in China, reporting a famine situation. 
On 1 October 1998, during a press conference, MSF gave a public explanation of the 
reasons for its withdrawal from North Korea, shared its concern on the outcome of 
socially disadvantaged children excluded by the system, and referred to the witness 
accounts of the North Korean refugees. It also called on the international community to 
stop delivering aid to North Korea whilst it was unable to check that the aid was really 
reaching the most vulnerable members of the population.
Throughout this whole period, MSF was confronted with the following 
dilemmas and questions: 
 Up to what point could MSF, to draw closer to a population in distress, accept 
to work without being able to apply the basic principles of humanitarian 
action: access to populations, free evaluation of needs, and supervision of the 
destination of its assistance? 
 Should MSF accept to work for a population oppressed by a totalitarian regime 
with the risk of its assistance serving to reinforce this oppression and support 
this regime?
 Whilst making public calls for emergency aid for the North Korean health 
system, was MSF not participating in reinforcing the regime? 
EPILOGUE:
In the years that followed, the French section, with the support of its internal research 
centre, continued to document the deviations of international aid delivered to North 
Korea and spoke out on this subject in the press on several different occasions. At 
the same time, it ran a programme of little operational stature (because it was partly 
clandestine) in an attempt to bring assistance to North Korean refugees in China or in 
transit through different countries in Asia and South Korea.

From 2001 to 2003, MSF France spoke out publicly several times to denounce the 
repression and forced repatriation suffered by these refugees in China, despite the 
presence of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR). As time 
passed, all MSF’s activities concerning North Korean refugees dwindled to a standstill. 
Meanwhile, in 2004, a visit by a delegation of MSF Holland to North Korea with the aim 
of preparing an exploratory mission led to nothing. 
In 2007, new alarms sounded on the food situation in North Korea, and a continuous 
influx of refugees arrived on the Sino-Korean border, inciting the Swiss section to 
launch an exploratory mission, then a process of re-opening a programme for North 
Korean refugees.
